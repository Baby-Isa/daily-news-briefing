In fact, however, Lorenzo ‘the Magnificent’ owned five portable firearms, which are listed in his 1492 postmortem inventory. Guns and gunpowder were controversial. In art and literature, they were labelled the Devil’s work, and were perceived as fundamentally unchivalrous. Yet within half a century, by the early months of 1539, Lorenzo’s descendant Duke Cosimo de’ Medici had 91 firearms in his Florentine storerooms. Somehow, in the courts of late-Renaissance Italy, the gun had become an acceptable, even valued, weapon. Beyond the palazzi of the peninsula’s towns and cities, guns were also making inroads into more ordinary lives. There were no standing armies in the 16th-century Italian states. Their wars were fought through temporary mobilisations, and with considerable help from companies of mercenaries. Yet as the 1506 provision that established the Florentine militia made clear, ‘little hope’ could be placed ‘in foreign and hired arms’. Far better to establish a new magistracy with ‘full authority and power’ to enlist men from Florentine territory, at least 10,000 of them. One in 10 of these men were to be equipped with firearms, and 500 handguns ( schioppetti ) should be kept in the Palazzo della Signoria (the city hall, now known as the Palazzo Vecchio) along with ‘such money as should be necessary for the schioppetti ’, presumably to purchase gunpowder and shot. Three or four companies ( bandiere ) entirely of schioppettieri might also be established.
