* ‘Sinking’ African megacities will expose seven million people to flooding by 2050
  Carbon Brief | 27 Nov 19:00 UTC
  Sinking land and rising seas could expose more than seven million people living in Africa’s coastal “megacities” to flooding by… The post ‘Sinking’ African megacities will expose seven million people to flooding by 2050 appeared first on Carbon Brief .
  FULL TEXT: Sinking land and rising seas could expose more than seven million people living in Africa’s coastal “megacities” to flooding by the middle of the century, according to new research. The analysis, published in Science Advances , combines satellite data with projections of sea level rise to assess the risk of flooding in 20 of Africa’s most populous coastal cities. The study finds that, under a moderate warming scenario, an “extreme sea level event” – when sea levels are unusually high due to short-term changes in weather and ocean conditions – could expose a million buildings and $180bn of assets to flooding. Lagos, the capital of Nigeria, and Alexandria in Egypt are the most vulnerable cities in the analysis. In these two cities alone, five million people and $150bn in assets could be exposed to flooding and the cities by mid-century. Human activity, such as pumping groundwater or drilling for fossil fuels, can cause the ground to sink, in a process called “ subsidence ”. Seismic or volcanic activity can also drive changes in the height of the land, causing it to either rise or fall. This “vertical land movement” can either alleviate or exacerbate the effects of sea level rise. The researchers combined more than 4,000 satellite images of Africa’s coastline to determine how quickly different regions are rising or sinking. They identified Lagos and Alexandria as “subsidence hotspots”, finding that the two cities are sinking at rates of 5.2mm and 6.3mm per year, respectively. This is “comparable to rates reported for south-east Asian megacities such as Jakarta, Manila and Ho Chi Minh City”, which have recorded “some of the highest subsidence rates yet observed”, according to the study. The authors combined these estimates with sea level rise projections from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s sixth assessment report under three different emissions scenarios , to assess the regional sea level rise across the African coastline. They used 2020 population data, building footprints and GDP per capita to estimate how much of each city would be exposed to these flooding events over the 21st century, if the city itself stays the same. While Lagos and Alexandria are the most vulnerable cities, the research finds that Djibouti City in Djibouti, Mogadishu in Somalia and Tripoli in Libya will face “minimal economic exposure due to their smaller populations and infrastructure bases”. Lead author Oluwaseyi Dasho , a PhD student at Virginia Tech , tells Carbon
