Courtesy of Far North Footage/Getty Images The French naturalist Jean-Baptiste Lamarck was 65 when his great treatise on the transformation of life was published. With Philosophie zoologique (1809), translated a century later as Zoological Philosophy , Lamarck intended to establish an entirely new science, one built on the idea that all living beings are constantly transforming themselves, from the tiniest microbes to the most complex mammals, gaining new forms and new abilities. It wasn’t the bodily structures of organisms that determined their ways of life, Lamarck said, but just the opposite: their ways of life in response to their circumstances gave rise to their changing bodily forms. ‘Can there be any consideration in natural history more important,’ he demanded, ‘or deserving of greater attention, than the one I have just set forth?’ (My translation.) But if you’ve heard anything at all about Lamarck, it’s probably how wrong he was about one animal in particular, the giraffe, and how this giraffe fiasco epitomises his wrongness about everything . Lamarck suggested that, by stretching to reach leaves in high tree branches, giraffes slightly elongated their necks, creating tiny changes that were then handed down to their offspring, accumulating over many generations to produce the long necks we see today. Around the turn of the 20th century, Lamarck’s giraffe idea became a laughing stock when biologists roundly rejected the notion that bodily changes undergone in the course of an individual lifetime could be inherited in the next generation. Illustration from Histoire naturelle, générale et particulière (1749-1804) by Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon. Courtesy the Linda Hall Library It wasn’t only his ideas about long-necked mammals that came under attack. Lamarck’s interest in giraffes was just a passing mention in the midst of his voluminous writings, which set out a revolutionary, field-founding theory: organisms, he claimed, transform themselves and, in doing so, they remake the world. That idea has drawn scorn for more than two centuries. Though Lamarck is often overlooked, his science was foundational to modern biology and informed the theory of evolution that Charles Darwin later developed.
