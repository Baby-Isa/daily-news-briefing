Weather.

Eighteen point nine millimetres of rain today, eighteen degrees to twenty-two. Twenty-two degrees. And pissing down. That is not rain, that is a warm shower you did not ask for and cannot turn off. Coat on, arrive sweating. Coat off, arrive soaked. No third door. This brief has been threatening you with today since Friday and for once it was not exaggerating — it undersold it. So: waterproof, nothing under it, accept your fate. Then the week takes the roof off the heat, twenty-three tomorrow down to sixteen by Thursday, rain more or less done. Today is the last warm wet one.

Economy.

The Federal Open Market Committee, the Fed's rate-setting body, meets Tuesday and Wednesday, the fifteenth and sixteenth of September. As flagged, the dovish case died on Friday: headline inflation unchanged at three point four percent, core up to two point four, a rise around ninety percent priced.

The mechanism: the committee lifts the federal funds target, the overnight rate banks lend each other at, for the first time this cycle. The intent: anchor expectations before an oil shock built on Hormuz reaches wage bargaining. The risk is that two schools read one dataset oppositely — the Phillips-curve view says a labour market still adding a hundred and sixty thousand jobs a month will generalise the shock, so tighten; the supply-shock view says rates cannot drill for oil, and tightening into a relative-price move buys weakness without touching the cause, with core producer prices at plus nought point two against plus nought point three expected still saying upstream pressure is narrow. The read-through: firmer dollar, weaker cable, a higher discount rate on the largest data-centre build ever attempted.

It is a Group of Seven pattern, not an American one: the Bank of Japan is expected at one and a quarter percent next week, the European Central Bank is already at two and a half. Three major banks tightening into one barrel.

Crude settled near a hundred and four dollars Friday. Speculators turned net long the yen for the first time since February: the carry trade unwinding ahead of the Bank of Japan.

Sam Altman says an OpenAI float this year would be ill-advised. Anthropic is still set for mid-October at a talked-of two trillion dollars: the two biggest private artificial-intelligence companies now disagree on when to take public money.

Business.

The safety chorus went mainstream. Dario Amodei called on AI firms to slow development, with a three-step plan behind it, and Sam Altman and Elon Musk both backed him, days after a researcher quit the industry over existential risk. Investors are sceptical: the labs asking to be constrained while their capital providers are not is the split to watch.

Mergers.

CVC's ten point seven billion euro take-private of Recordati, the Italian rare-disease drugmaker, has hit a shareholder revolt: investors call fifty-one euros twenty-nine a share a lowball and say they are being pressured to sell. CVC already controls forty-seven percent through an existing vehicle, which is the point: the minority has no leverage and knows it.

Science.

Base editing in human embryos can now reach every cell, removing the mosaicism problem that made it clinically useless — but it still produces unpredictable genetic changes alongside the intended one. That is the gate, and it stays shut.

Gadgets.

Thin day. Apple's A20 Pro has set a single-core record on Geekbench: a two-nanometre part beating desktop Intel Core i9 and AMD Ryzen 9 chips by up to thirty-two percent, shipping in the iPhone 18 Pro now on pre-order. A phone chip outrunning desktop silicon on single thread is this generation's headline fact. Nothing else shipped, changed price or died.

Energy.

The Atlantic has produced no hurricane at all this season, the longest such start since satellite records began, despite warm water. El Nino's wind shear is suppressing them — the same El Nino driving Indonesia's worst fire season this century. One system, opposite signs: a quiet hurricane season is a symptom, not a reprieve.

Editorial picks.

One, and the notable one: the Economist's Weekend Intelligence is back after a summer off, which changes what Sundays carry from here. The episode is Shire Folk. It argues that fifteen years of British media policy keeping Tommy Robinson off television and out of print preceded rather than prevented rallies now drawing tens of thousands, and traces how influential Americans came to see Britain a particular way. The case it argues against is that coverage amplifies. Ask for the full expansion.
