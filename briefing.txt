Politics. One. Kazakhstan, the vote flagged for today. The first Kurultai election replaces the two-chamber parliament with one chamber of a hundred and forty-five seats, elected entirely by proportional party list in a single national constituency. Turnout under eighteen percent by ten in the morning. The load-bearing fact: the ruling Amanat party is not on the ballot. It dissolved itself in June into Adilet, created this year, which now fields the biggest list of the seven running. A rebrand, not a contest. Journalists went in under criminal cases and travel bans. S&P raised Kazakhstan to triple-B on Friday.

Two. Britain. Andy Burnham will give mayors power to call in local planning decisions and force through large developments, plus a say over national housing funding, in proposals due next week. Councils lose the veto on housing.

Three. The Palestinian vice president confirmed November legislative elections stand, with Hamas barred from contesting under the Gaza disarmament plan. In Tanzania, opposition leader Tundu Lissu's treason trial opened; he calls it a political case.

Conflict. One. Iran. As flagged, Bessent's sanctions package lands tomorrow. Economy. The trade rupture. Fifty percent American tariffs took effect Saturday on roughly twenty billion dollars of Canadian goods, five percent of Canada's exports south. Carney walked away and retaliates from the eighth of September on American steel and electronics.

The mechanism: Section 232 duties at fifty percent, met by Canadian counter-duties three weeks out, no talks scheduled. The intended effect: coercion. Washington's ask, per its trade representative, was provinces lifting bans on American alcohol plus dairy and procurement access, so the tariff is leverage on an economy sending three-quarters of its exports to one buyer. The risks split economists. The optimal-tariff reading says a large importer can shift the terms of trade in its favour; the standard reading says incidence falls mostly on American importers and both price levels rise, with the added cost that the North American pact itself is now openly questioned. The read-through: goods disinflation reverses at the margin into a Fed whose own minutes already flagged a possible need to raise, tightening the path for dollar assets rather than loosening it.

Second. Nvidia has told customers server prices rise more than fifteen percent on systems shipping early next year, including Vera Rubin and Grace Blackwell configurations. The cause is memory. Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron hold the pricing power, and the largest company in the cycle cannot absorb it. The same squeeze reached consumers this week: Amazon raised Echo, Kindle and Fire TV prices by up to sixty percent citing memory and storage costs, cheapest devices hit hardest. Read that as the AI build-out taxing everything downstream, compressing margins outside the chipmakers rather than inside them.

Third. Korea's Kospi is down thirty percent from its June peak, wiping out retail investors drawn in by a deliberate deregulation push, and now a political problem for President Lee. India's reliance on Russian crude hit an all-time high, its hedge against Gulf disruption.

Business. Chinese carmakers, having taken the electric segment, now target full hybrids, Geely and Changan posting strong numbers against the Japanese incumbents. Exports tell the same story: one electric car sold abroad for every two at home in July, against one in five a year ago, domestic sales down twelve percent. Export necessity, not opportunity. Six EU states want a bloc-wide windfall tax on oil profits swollen by the war.
