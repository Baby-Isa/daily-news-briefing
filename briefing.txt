Politics and government.

United States, midterms Tuesday. Times and Siena polls have Democrats ahead in Senate races in Michigan, Texas, Kansas, Maine and Ohio, with an edge in Alaska and a tie in Iowa. Polls have burned them before. A judge dismissed the Justice Department suit seeking to block Alaska certifying its Senate result if the Republican incumbent loses. In Michigan, Democratic congresswoman Haley Stevens endorsed the Republican, Mike Rogers, over her own party's nominee El-Sayed; Trump invited her to switch parties. Senator Chris Murphy had a stroke and is expected to recover fully.

UK. As flagged, the King addresses Commonwealth leaders on slavery in Antigua Tuesday. Burnham is open to a formal apology; Badenoch is against; the Guardian reports Britain argued hard against reparations reaching the summit agenda. Britain would spend four hundred fifty million pounds a year on European Union student places to join the Made in Europe scheme. The home secretary is to announce rules making some migrants wait ten years or more to settle.

Israel. The elections chief says the result is valid; the Shin Bet foreign-funding inquiry rests on suspicions, not findings. Right-wing protesters in Haifa demand a government without Arab parties.

Poland: the European Commission backs parliament's right to appoint constitutional court judges; Tusk says he will not back down. Bulgaria elected its first female president, Iliana Iotova. India: tens of thousands rallied in Delhi demanding the election chief resign over the removal of one hundred thirty million names from the voter rolls.

East Africa: Kenya's Ebola contacts, forty-six, leave quarantine Tuesday, when its candidate Okowa also faces the International Court of Justice vote.

Conflict and security.

Iran. Trump says the war ends very soon, one way or the other: a deal, or what he calls the hammer. Iran warns a new round could outlast the last. Iranian negotiators had a third day with Qatari mediators in Doha. The UAE hopes for a breakthrough but is braced for the worst. Qatar's gas tanker was attacked in the Gulf; the UAE and Saudi Arabia condemned it. Yamaha pulled out of next weekend's Qatar MotoGP. The post-midterm strike decision we flagged is now a day away.

Yemen. Saudi-led strikes hit Hodeidah, with casualties reported. The UN demands an investigation into the death of a staff member in Houthi custody.

Ukraine. The Oleshky truce, the war's longest, ended Sunday night, as flagged. Ukraine has resumed refinery strikes, reportedly two oil sites, defying Trump's pressure; the Kremlin says Kyiv is ignoring Washington. Air-raid alert apps were hacked on both sides; Kyiv's briefly showed false nuclear and chemical warnings.


International affairs.

Disasters, to know: a Learjet crashed near Palm Beach, Florida, killing nine after reporting icing; India evacuates thousands ahead of Cyclone Murasu, its second in a week.

The United Nations peacekeeping mission in Western Sahara ended after thirty-five years when the Security Council failed to renew it. Morocco welcomed that; the Polisario Front called it unacceptable. Canada's Carney gave the European Union six months to define what associate membership means. Politico reports diplomats fear Trump, after a midterm loss, could pull more troops from Europe or cut NATO funding.

Economy and markets.

AI. The global sell-off eased after the Nasdaq's worst month in over a year. Amazon, Alphabet and Microsoft say they will keep pouring money into data centres even as investors grow impatient. The Financial Times argues AI mania cannot survive higher rates: a higher discount rate cuts the present value of distant profits, and debt-funded builders suffer first. The other reading: hyperscalers fund capex from cash flow, so rates bite mainly on the leveraged fringe. With the Fed at three seventy-five to four and a December hike live, the first reading is being tested.

Fuel. The FT says petrol is set for a record on election eve, with the average over Trump's second term past the Biden era. Mechanism: a war-driven oil supply shock. Central banks must choose between looking through it and tightening, and three point seven percent inflation weakens the case for looking through. Phillips-curve economists say slack must cool prices; supply-shock economists say tightening just adds unemployment.

Memory. Sam Altman met Samsung leadership to secure more RAM for data centres, per Tom's Hardware, while Apple's new chief executive, Ternus, faces his first earnings with memory costs surging. Demand still looks tight, against Kioxia's stall warning.

Korea. South Korea's president proposed a record budget for next year that doubles spending on AI and chips, a fiscal bet on the cycle the Nasdaq is doubting.

Calendar: Australia's central bank decides Tuesday, with a hike live.

Mobico, owner of National Express, agreed a recommended cash offer from Autobuses de Leon, owned by the Cosmen family's CMC Group and fund manager Asterion. Science and technology.

NASA is taking commercial proposals to boost Hubble's orbit; without one its time runs out in the early to mid twenty thirties. Swift, another NASA telescope, is about to fall to Earth. A simulated Mars crew emerged after three hundred seventy-eight days in isolation in Houston. The FT reports safety breaches at leading AI labs raise doubts they can keep systems in check.
