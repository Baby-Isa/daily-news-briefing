Economy. The yen. Tokyo's intervention worked, and the bill is visible: the yen is at its strongest since February, and the data suggest Japan sold US Treasuries to fund what was a record operation. Four parts. Mechanism: the finance ministry sold dollars for yen at record scale, liquidating reserves to do it, while Bessent publicly pushed the Bank of Japan to tighten and pronounced the Abenomics era over. Intent: kill the imported-inflation spiral a hundred-and-fifty-seven yen was feeding, and move the work from intervention, which is finite, to rate rises, which are not. Risks: selling Treasuries to defend a currency lifts the US long end, the one thing Washington cannot afford with this deficit, and economists split on whether a Bank of Japan hike unwinds the carry trade as an orderly drift or an August 2024 rush. Read-through: a stronger yen and higher Japanese rates raise the funding cost of the leveraged dollar-asset trade, a direct headwind for the AI-capex names, and it compounds the reserve-manager diversification we have tracked since Norway's oil fund. Oil is closing on a hundred dollars on shipping attacks and eroding inventories. That runs into Friday's CPI and the FOMC on the fifteenth, where after August's 162,000 payrolls the market prices a hike, not a cut. The energy shock is the fault line: Phillips-curve readings say a labour market this tight generalises it into wages, so tighten; supply-shock readings say rates cannot fix a relative price and you buy a recession. China put fifty-four billion dollars into state banks and insurers: recapitalisation to make room for lending, not demand for households, so the underlying problem is untouched.
