Weather. Right, today is the wet one. Sixteen to twenty-one, cloud the colour of dishwater, six and a half millimetres of rain and wind up to eight metres a second, enough to turn an umbrella inside out. Waterproof, not the nice jacket. And then it stops. Completely. Not one drop for the next five days, three of them clear sky, twenty-three on Tuesday, proper warmth in the back half of September. Five dry days in a row in this country is basically a rumour. Get soaked today, and the rest of the week pays you back.

Politics. One. Trump has signed the Russia sanctions bill two days after the House passed it: sweeping sanctions on Moscow, Iranian sanctions extended to twenty thirty-one, and power to impose additional tariffs on any country breaking the embargo. That last clause is the live one: it points at India, which buys Russian crude, and it lands days before Xi Jinping reaches Washington. Two. He has barred two American news networks and a news site from the White House for reporting what he called fake news. Three. Russia's parliamentary vote runs to Sunday; requests for emergency help leaving the country are up eightfold since July on fear of mobilisation. Four. Germany votes tomorrow in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, where Merz's own position rides on the result, and in Berlin, where the campaign turns on a left-wing plan to expropriate apartment blocks to force rents down. Morocco votes Wednesday.

Conflict. One. Pakistan. A suicide bomber and gunmen stormed police headquarters at Kohat: at least thirty-one dead, including sixteen officers. Two. The Gulf. Saudi authorities issued and then withdrew danger alerts for Riyadh overnight, with explosions reported in the capital. A newspaper investigation counts twenty-two American military deaths in the Iran war, four more than the Pentagon admits; the defence secretary denies it. Three. Ukraine. Ukrainian forces say their Lyman operation has pushed Russian units off the approach to Sloviansk, and guided bombs killed two council staff in a block of flats in Sumy. Poland's Tusk warns of deliberately accidental Russian strikes on allies, meant to show that Article Five exists only on paper, and France has ordered critical infrastructure prepared for drone and cyber attack. Four. American intelligence came close to boarding a Chinese ship over cargo an analyst's chatbot had hallucinated.

International. Three to be aware of: an Indonesian ferry capsized with a hundred and twenty-six missing; methanol poisoning has killed forty-nine in Nigeria; floods shut Tunis. Trump says he has an agreement with Denmark and Greenland giving Washington permanent authority over the island's security and a veto over military activity or sensitive investment by American adversaries, China among them. Copenhagen has published no terms.

Economy. One. The past week's tightening is now showing up in currencies rather than in rates. Tokyo is reported to have run a rate check on the yen, the step immediately before intervention, because the Bank of Japan's move to a thirty-one-year high has not held the currency. Mexico's peso has slid toward seventeen point three to the dollar, broken by the Fed rather than anything domestic: when the risk-free dollar rate rises, capital stops paying for carry. Two. The financing of the artificial-intelligence build-out is where strain is appearing. OpenAI's own projections have it burning two hundred and eighty billion dollars by twenty thirty, funding infrastructure into falling prices; Oracle's eighteen-billion-dollar data-centre debt is under pressure; and investors ahead of Anthropic's float doubt its revenue survives cheaper rivals. The spending itself is not in doubt. The question is who carries it, and the answer is increasingly debt and vendor finance rather than cash flow, which turns an equity story into a credit one. Credit is where cycles break. Three. Britain. More than one point one million low-income renting families face a housing shortfall averaging a hundred and fifty-eight pounds a week on a typical two-bedroom home, with a call to lift the housing allowance freeze in next month's budget. The freeze is a real-terms cut that saves the Treasury more the faster rents rise, which is why it is both hard to defend and hard to give up.

Business. One. India. The Tata Sons board is in revolt over reappointing its chairman and over a demand that it list publicly, which would open the books of the country's most powerful private conglomerate. Two. American nuclear construction is separating winners from losers. Bechtel has quit the Gates-backed reactor project and filed notice of two hundred layoffs; Holtec presses on with small modular reactors at Palisades in Michigan but has shelved the share sale meant to fund it. Investor conditions, not engineering. Three. Kenya's government has promised to investigate its state-to-state fuel import deal after Uganda's Museveni publicly named the middlemen taking a margin out of it, which bears on Kenyan fuel prices and the shilling.

Deals. One. Ingredion has agreed to buy Tate and Lyle for three point six billion dollars, taking a listed British household name and its sweetener business to an American ingredients group. Two. Paramount is in talks to settle the antitrust action by a dozen Democratic-run states, the last obstacle to its hundred and eleven billion dollar tie-up with Warner Brothers Discovery; the American communications regulator has separately cleared Saudi, Emirati and Qatari sovereign funds to hold nearly half the merged company's equity.

Science. One. NASA's lunar orbiter has photographed a crater that was not there before, roughly two hundred metres across, from an impact of a size expected about once a century, with before and after images of the same ground. Two. Google's Gemini model broke into three real companies in a security exercise, then halted on its own: the third frontier laboratory in a week to report real intrusion work by its model.

Gadgets. Clicks has dated the Communicator, the Android phone with a physical keyboard under a four-inch screen: shipping in December, nearly a year after it was shown. Twelve gigabytes of memory rather than eight, and Android seventeen with updates promised to Android twenty. The four-hundred-and-ninety-nine dollar launch price ends this month; after that, six hundred and forty-nine, a thirty percent rise before a unit ships. Separately, capacity is shifting away from the cheap flash memory in everyday electronics, and undersupply there is now called severe.

Energy. Saudi Aramco has told European refiners they will get no crude in October under long-term contracts, after the drone strike closed its pipeline to the Red Sea, and is routing barrels to Asia through Hormuz instead. Macron is convening an emergency Group of Seven meeting on energy prices and says France is working to reopen the strait. Set that against Thursday's Bank of England hold, explicitly conditioned on energy prices: an October shortfall in Europe is the shock that makes that hold temporary.

Health. Every American state has signed up to the plan aligning Medicaid drug prices with those paid in other rich countries, announced before the midterms with the mechanics undisclosed, which is where most-favoured-nation pricing has failed before.

British Airways returns to Tanzania after thirteen years away. Xpeng takes its five-metre G nine L crossover to the Paris show next month, aimed at export markets. Chainlink: a one point two billion dollar tokenised treasury fund is now open to thirty-five million retail accounts, and tokenised shares can be pledged for loans. Agronomics: nothing on the company; in the sector an Indian biotech has bought a Japanese fermentation manufacturer for capacity, and the warning that aligning British food standards with Europe costs the country its cell-agriculture lead is four days old with no government answer.
