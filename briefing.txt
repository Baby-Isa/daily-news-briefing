Weather. Correction first, and it is mine. Twice this week I promised you a Thursday soaking: seven millimetres on Tuesday, twelve yesterday, the day that breaks the pattern. Thursday has turned up with three point two. Three! The forecast did not escalate, it evaporated, and I marched you into an umbrella you will barely open. Thirteen degrees now, twenty by mid-afternoon, fair, light breeze, dress for mild and ignore me. After today, genuinely nothing: dry and cloudy, highs pinned between eighteen and twenty-one all the way to Tuesday, barely a millimetre across six days. There is no wet day this week. Today is as wet as it gets.

Economy. The Federal Reserve has raised interest rates. The thread we have run since the eleventh closes: a rise was priced, the hold condition had failed, and last night it happened. One, the mechanism. The Open Market Committee voted twelve to nothing to lift the federal funds target by twenty-five basis points - a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point - to a range of three and three-quarter to four percent, the first increase since July twenty twenty-three. Kevin Warsh's first rise as chairman, under four months in, from a committee the president has spent months demanding cut. Two, the intent. Warsh said inflation is too high and has been for too long: tighten enough and the oil shock cannot pass out of fuel and food into wages and expectations. Three, the risk. The Phillips-curve reading has won the decision - a labour market this tight generalises a relative-price shock, so you lean against it - but the supply-shock objection is not thereby wrong: no interest rate reopens a Saudi pipeline, so the Fed may buy an output loss and get no disinflation for it. Not a beaten argument, then, but the risk the Fed has chosen to run. Four, the read-through. A cycle, not a one-off: sixteen of the eighteen participants project at least one further rise, four project two, implying four to four and a quarter percent by year end. Warsh submitted no projection of his own. For the portfolio: a firmer dollar, sterling weaker against dollar-denominated holdings, and a higher discount rate on the artificial-intelligence capital-expenditure trade, the cost of capital rising just as capex commitments peak. The yen was jolted and the Bank of Japan now meets with a far higher bar for defending it, making a Japanese rise likelier. The president's answer: demand cuts fast, and threaten to end trade with Mexico and Europe. The Bank of England decides at midday, a hold expected, British inflation back up to three point one percent on fuel.

Conflict. One. Saudi Arabia and Yemen. The Houthis deny aiming at Mecca and claim they downed a Saudi fighter; Riyadh promises deterrent measures. Their Red Sea advance is Riyadh's worst setback in Yemen in years: proxies it armed and trained overrun, missile defences low. Trump now says the war is near its end and claims direct talks with Tehran. Hormuz crossings fell to three vessels yesterday; oil eased after Saudi Arabia routed more crude to Asian refiners through Oman. Two. Ukraine, day one thousand six hundred and sixty-seven. Ukrainian drones halted two more Rosneft refineries, so three of Russia's six largest diesel producers are shut or sharply cut. Three. International. Business. One. Two. Amazon has contracted Generac for up to eight billion dollars of data-centre generators and taken a warrant over its shares, which rose forty percent: the power bottleneck solved on site. Three. OpenAI disclosed six further safety incidents and a reporting system for model misbehaviour, as King Charles convened technology chiefs in Scotland and called the field's pace deeply concerning.

Science. One. Researchers have replaced nearly half the brain volume of mice with human cells, giving a mouse a working human cortex: a far better model for conditions such as cerebral palsy, and an ethics question the field has not answered. Two. New Horizons data suggests liquid nitrogen has flowed across Pluto's surface in the recent past, rising through cracks in the Sputnik Planitia glacier.

Gadgets. Snap's Specs went on general sale yesterday: standalone augmented-reality glasses, no phone, no tethered computer, see-through waveguide lenses placing graphics in the room in front of you, plus a new assistant, Specs Intelligence, that also runs on phone and desktop. Two thousand one hundred and ninety-five dollars, on sale now in America, Britain and France, cellular tied to one American network, the first production run deliberately capped at a hundred thousand units. Four hours of battery, twenty with the case, fifty-one degrees of field of view, wide for glasses and narrow for a headset. What the money buys is being early, at the price of two premium phones. Everything else this morning was iteration.

Energy. One. Global fossil-fuel emissions are set to fall about half a percent this year despite the Hormuz crisis: coal demand is up one point two percent, but falling oil and gas more than offset it. The shock is destroying demand faster than it is switching fuel. Two. India's power-sector emissions have been flat for two years, the first stretch without coal-power growth in over fifty years, though steel and cement pushed total emissions up three point seven percent. Three. Washington has finalised the repeal of carbon limits on coal and gas power stations, and proposes stripping the rest.

Health. England will fund Enhertu on the health service for a form of incurable advanced breast cancer after agreeing a price: about a thousand women a year gain a life-extending drug.
