Turning to the economy, and the story that deserves the deeper treatment today is the yen. The mechanism: Japan's currency weakened another one percent on Monday to around one hundred fifty-nine to the dollar, erasing roughly half the gains from the joint US-Japan currency intervention on the thirty-first of July, even after US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent repeated a "whatever it takes" pledge to support it. The Bank of Japan's July summary flagged upside price risks and signalled a possible rate hike as soon as September. The intended effect is straightforward: a weaker yen has been feeding import-cost inflation in Japan for months, and Japan just posted its first current-account deficit in eighteen months, so both the intervention and the prospect of a hike are aimed at halting depreciation before it further erodes real incomes and central bank credibility. The unintended risks are where it gets interesting: intervention alone, without a matching rate move, tends to buy only temporary relief - which is exactly what's happening, as today's reporting on the Treasury's "limited firepower" suggests markets are already testing the Bank of Japan's resolve. If the Bank of Japan does hike in September while the Federal Reserve stays on a more cautious path, that widens the case for yen strength quickly rather than gradually, which is precisely the scenario carry-trade unwinds are made of. The read-through for a portfolio dominated by US equities: yen volatility is usually a secondary risk unless it's sharp enough to force a rapid unwind of yen-funded carry trades into US and global risk assets, something markets have handled clumsily before. It's also a reminder that the Bank of Japan, not just the Federal Reserve, is now a genuine swing factor in global rate expectations worth watching into September.

Turning to health and medicine, and the dominant story is domestic US policy: Trump has signed an executive order calling for fewer childhood vaccinations, including splitting the combined measles-mumps-rubella vaccine into three separate shots given months apart, and has directed the Attorney General to sue states that don't offer broad religious and medical exemptions from school immunisation requirements. The administration frames this as expanding parental choice and addressing what it calls an over-loaded vaccine schedule; mainstream medical bodies and previous court rulings have rejected the underlying claims, including the president's repeated assertion, contradicted by decades of safety data, that the MMR vaccine is dangerous and linked to autism.
