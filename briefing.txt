Economy. One, the tariff thread. Mechanism: Trump answered Canada's twenty billion dollar retaliation not with tariffs but outright import bans — dairy, motorcycles, cheese, most alcohol — plus higher rates elsewhere and a plan to strike Canadian goods from federal purchasing. Intent: a ban forgoes the revenue and attacks volume directly, so the pain lands faster and in politically chosen sectors. Risk: bans are far harder to unwind than tariffs and invite mirroring, and Carney has conceded the cost while refusing to move, so Ottawa is planning for a long war. Read-through: the tariff channel stays alive in Friday's inflation print. Two. The Federal Reserve. Brent touched ninety-nine dollars forty-nine overnight, and China's factory-gate prices picked up in August on energy — the oil shock reaching a major producer's pipeline, which is how it reaches American import prices. Friday's consumer price index is two days out, the meeting on the fifteenth and sixteenth follows. Waller's condition was a hold if inflation cools and a possible rise if not; the market prices a rise. The disagreement: the Phillips-curve reading says a tight labour market generalises an energy shock, so tighten, while the supply-shock reading says that is tightening into a relative-price move you cannot control. Three. Japan. Bessent dared traders to short the yen after the record intervention, while rising Japanese yields revive the repatriation question: capital coming home tightens global long rates with no central bank acting.

Science and technology. One. OpenAI says its agents solved part of the Navier-Stokes problem — one of the seven Millennium Prize problems, on fluid flow — in eighty-eight hours. Mathematicians immediately disputed what was proved and how much rests on existing human work. If it holds, it is the first machine contribution to a major open problem. Two.
