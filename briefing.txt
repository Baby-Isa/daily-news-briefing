Lead item. Iran. The Bessent measures flagged as due for over a week have landed. Trump announced what he calls the most crushing economic operation ever taken against any country: secondary sanctions on any country or firm giving Iran any type of lifeline. The real target is China, which buys most Iranian crude; the threat is loss of dollar access for the banks and refiners that settle it. The UAE went first, halting all trade and finance with Tehran. Hormuz has not moved: transits show no rise, and Gulf supertankers now rent at half a million dollars a day.

Economy. Gross US federal debt passed forty trillion dollars, double a decade ago. The consequential move was the Treasury doubling its long-end buybacks.

The mechanism. From the ninth of September to the fourth of November, Treasury buys back at least four billion dollars per operation of ten to thirty-year paper, up from two, funded with shorter issuance.

The intent. Cap the long end. Thirty-year yields had reached five point two percent and the ten-year four point six five, highest since two thousand and seven. Both fell on the news, nine and six basis points.

The risks. Two readings. The benign one is plumbing: four billion is small, and it buys illiquid off-the-run bonds to restore market function. The other is activist issuance, shortening the government's average maturity to suppress a price signal while the Fed sits split on inflation. That is fiscal dominance by another name, and it rebuilds term premium later rather than removing it.

The read-through. Long yields are the discount rate under the AI capex cycle, so a capped long end supports mega-cap valuations directly. But if this reads as Treasury doing monetary policy, the dollar softens, cutting the sterling value of dollar assets independently of the companies.
