Weather. Fourteen to twenty-two degrees in Ruislip today, three millimetres of rain, barely any wind. Twenty-two degrees in mid-September is your mate who announces he's leaving the party and then stands by the door for an hour. It isn't summer, it isn't autumn, it's a temperature with no opinions and a shower that'll be over before you've finished swearing about it. So you'll take the jacket, you won't wear the jacket, and you'll carry the bloody thing round all day like a hostage. Rest of the week, mild and mostly dry, highs between seventeen and twenty-two. Thursday is the one to plan around: seven point three millimetres, genuinely wet.

Economy. The Federal Open Market Committee convenes this morning for two days and announces at two in the afternoon New York time tomorrow, six in the evening here. Nothing is decided today, and tomorrow's brief closes before the announcement, so the result reaches you Thursday. The mechanism: the federal funds target, with a quarter-point rise roughly ninety percent priced, because Governor Waller's condition for holding was cooler inflation and August's consumer price index did not deliver it: headline unchanged at three point four percent, core up to two point four. The intent: stop an oil shock becoming an inflation expectation, by tightening enough that fuel costs cannot pass into wages. The risk is where economists split: the Phillips-curve reading says the labour market is tight enough to generalise the shock, so tighten; the supply-shock reading says rates cannot reopen a Saudi pipeline, so you buy the output loss without the disinflation. Read-through: a rise firms the dollar, weakens cable against dollar-denominated holdings and lifts the discount rate on the artificial-intelligence capital-expenditure trade, while a hold reads as capitulation to a president demanding the world's lowest rates. Watch the dot plot as much as the rate. The eve got worse, not better. Brent is near a hundred and eight dollars against a hundred and four at Friday's close, on the shut Saudi East-West pipeline, and it fed the long end: US ten-year yields touched five percent, first time since twenty twenty-three, Japan's passed three. Term premium repricing, not the policy rate. US manufacturers report fresh input-cost inflation from tariffs, the war and AI-driven component scarcity: the supply side the Fed cannot reach.
