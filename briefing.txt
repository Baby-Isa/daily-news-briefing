Economy. The Bank of Japan has raised its policy rate to a thirty-one-year high, the second of the decisions flagged yesterday. One, the mechanism: a widely expected rise, following an unusual public campaign by the American Treasury secretary for tighter Japanese policy, which makes it the rare tightening a foreign government lobbied for. Two, the intent: narrow the gap with American rates, stop the yen bleeding value, and choke off imported energy inflation at source. Three, the risk: Japan's ten-year yield is already above three percent, so lifting the short rate into a long end that is repricing anyway threatens disorderly moves in the developed world's largest government debt stock relative to output. And the competing reading matters, because yesterday's American rise raised the bar; this may still not hold the currency, which is why Tokyo keeps promising orderly moves, the standard pre-intervention formula. Four, the read-through: two of the three largest central banks tightening inside a week lifts the global discount rate together, and a firmer yen erodes the cheap funding behind a great deal of risk-taking, the artificial-intelligence capital expenditure trade included. The Bank of England held at three and three-quarter percent, a sixth consecutive hold, warning that sustained high energy prices make a rise likelier later. Britain is the outlier of the three, waiting six weeks out from the budget, and sterling loses the rate argument in both directions. Brazil cut for a fifth straight meeting, to thirteen and three-quarter percent, two weeks from its presidential first round.

Science. One.
