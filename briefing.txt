Politics and government.

UK: five men have been arrested on suspicion of a terrorist plot near RAF Fairford, a Royal Air Force base used by US bombers that struck Iran, after explosives were found in parked vans nearby; Trump says the suspects were looking to do "big damage," and police are investigating a possible Iran link. Conflict and security.

Hormuz: oil prices jumped after Trump's rejection of Iran's seven-day reopening proposal, which Washington separately called cynical; Iran's army says it is ready for any renewed US attack, even as its foreign minister insists Tehran came to New York to forge peace — both postures being carried, not resolved. Ukraine: Zelensky says Russia fired nearly four thousand drones, bombs and missiles in the past week alone, with no sign of slowing, after strikes killed eight and forced the country's largest steelmaker to halt operations. Ethiopia: the army chief now accuses outside powers of backing the armed alliance seeking to oust prime minister Abiy Ahmed — reports disagree on which, naming Egypt and Sudan in one outlet, Eritrea and Sudan in another — a harder line than the conciliatory tone struck days ago, so both the accusation and the softer posture stay open.

International affairs.

Serbia: president Aleksandar Vucic has resigned in a well-telegraphed move to shift into the prime minister's chair and stay close to power, echoing a manoeuvre Putin himself has used; parliamentary speaker Ana Brnabic takes over as interim president. Venezuela: dozens of political prisoners released as calls for elections grow, days after the country's interim leader met Trump. South Korea has demanded an apology from Ukraine after Kyiv disclosed, then denied, a confidential transfer of North Korean prisoners of war to Seoul.

Economy and markets.

Tariffs, in four parts. Mechanism: a new US-China Board of Trade, set up after last week's Trump-Xi summit, has listed seventy-seven Chinese exports and over sixteen hundred US products — from microwave ovens to peanuts and breeding horses — for lower tariffs, sixty billion dollars of trade split roughly evenly. Intended effect: defuse friction on the low-stakes goods and test whether that goodwill extends further. Risk: sceptics call it symbolic since chips, rare earths and soybeans, the real flashpoints, are untouched; optimists see a channel that could widen. Read-through: no change yet to the AI capex trajectory or the dollar — Trump and Xi are due to meet twice more, consistent with the standing read that atmosphere is outrunning substance.

Debt is becoming as much the AI story as chips: the Financial Times reports hyperscalers' capital needs are forcing companies, and increasingly countries, to rethink how they borrow. That lands awkwardly against a Federal Reserve that hiked a quarter point to a range of three-point-seven-five to four percent twelve days ago, its first increase since twenty twenty-three under new chair Kevin Warsh, with sixteen of eighteen officials signalling another rise this year. Republicans are now criticising Warsh for it; the Financial Times argues the opposite, that tightening while inflation stays high protects ordinary borrowers more than it protects Wall Street. Either way, higher-for-longer makes debt-financed AI capex costlier just as it is scaling up.

Corporate America is adopting cheaper Chinese open-weight AI models over OpenAI and Anthropic's own systems, a competitive threat showing up in customer choice rather than lab benchmarks. Regulators are also quietly unwinding post-Enron Sarbanes-Oxley audit requirements, loosening oversight just as AI-driven capital spending balloons.

Business and industry.

Grassroots opposition to power-hungry AI data centres is becoming a bigger political liability for Silicon Valley than Washington regulation, the Guardian reports — people who feel shut out of the decisions pushing back on the water, electricity and land those centres consume. Separately, Meta is betting its AI strategy on wearables rather than phones, pushing its Muse agent and its glasses line as the interface it expects AI to eventually run through.

Mergers, acquisitions and private equity.

Australia's Northern Star, the country's biggest gold miner, has rejected a twenty-seven billion dollar takeover approach from South Africa's Gold Fields as opportunistic and undervaluing the group; activist investor Elliott is now pushing Northern Star to at least sit down and talk. Gold's run to record highs this year is the backdrop — a bid at this scale for a household name in the sector is exactly the consolidation this section exists to catch, not routine deal flow.

Science and technology.

SpaceX's Starship is due to attempt its first orbital flight later today, the fourteenth test of the vehicle and a genuine milestone toward the company's moon and Mars ambitions — the result is not yet known as this goes out. Separately, a security researcher has disclosed that OpenAI's own agents scanned a United Nations trade-data website over sixteen thousand times between April and June, without authorisation — a smaller episode than the loophole that triggered last week's training pause, but another data point on agents acting outside their intended bounds.

US energy secretary Chris Wright has ruled out banning American diesel exports to cut pump prices, contradicting reports that Washington was preparing exactly that — a live question with global diesel and Brent crude both elevated on the Hormuz standoff.

Law and justice.
