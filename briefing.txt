Economy and markets.

One. The fiscal response to the war oil premium is now a pattern. Thailand is weighing a cut to oil tax, Australia's opposition wants fuel excise cut while crude is high, Britain's Liberal Democrats want ten pence off fuel duty, Germany has already cut. The mechanism is the tax wedge on motor fuel, lowered to hold the pump price down while crude carries a conflict premium. The intent is to stop an imported price shock passing into headline inflation and from there into wages. The risk is the standard one: a duty cut is a demand subsidy, so it supports consumption of the scarce thing, and it is far easier to cut than restore, so the fiscal cost outlives the shock. Read-through: it flatters headline inflation now and props it up later, and it complicates the Bank of England's hold at three and three-quarter per cent, conditioned explicitly on energy prices, five weeks from the budget.

Two. The Federal Reserve and the Bank of England are both pressing prime brokers on bank exposure to trading firms, after losses at an artificial-intelligence-focused hedge fund. The concern is not the fund, it is how much bank balance sheet sits behind the artificial intelligence trade.

Three. Japan. The yen is exposed with Tokyo on holiday after the Bank of Japan disappointed expectations. That is the headline and all of it.

Four. Firmus, the Nvidia-backed Australian data-centre operator, opens an institutional bookbuild on the sixth of October to raise about five billion dollars, listing on the twenty-second. One of Australia's largest floats, and more evidence the build-out is funded by markets and debt, not cash flow. SoftBank separately seeks over eleven billion dollars of high-yield bonds against its OpenAI position.

Science and technology.

One. Venus probably ate its own moon. New modelling answers why Earth's near twin has no satellite: it captured a rocky one, which spiralled in and was absorbed.

Two. Satellite records across forty-seven years put ice loss from Greenland and Antarctica at about twelve and a half trillion tons.

Gadgets and consumer tech. The optical drive supply chain has killed two products outright. Naim's Uniti Star and PS Audio's Super Audio compact disc transport are discontinued because the mechanisms inside them are no longer made: the parent of Denon and Marantz stopped supplying third parties, the obvious substitute then went out of production too, and component costs have roughly doubled. Fitting another needs new control code and physical rework, which is why they died rather than changed supplier. No replacement, no price, no date. American compact disc revenue rose nearly fifty-nine per cent in the first half of this year.
