Economy. Start with oil, because two facts collided. Hormuz transits hit zero, and American strategic petroleum reserves are at their lowest in over forty years. The mechanism: the buffer that normally absorbs a supply shock has already been spent, so the next barrel of shortfall goes straight to price. The intent behind those draws was to cap pump prices and keep headline inflation down through an election cycle, and it worked while it lasted. The risk is that a supply shock now passes untouched into headline consumer price inflation, which puts the Federal Reserve in the textbook bind: standard Phillips-curve logic says look through a relative price shock and do not tighten into it, while the 2021 experience says expectations unanchor faster than the models assume. Read-through: cuts get priced out, real rates stay higher for longer, and the artificial intelligence capital expenditure cycle is financed at the margin by exactly that. Second, Japan. Second-quarter growth came in at nought point three percent, missing forecasts, with both consumption and capital spending sagging. That weakens the case for the September Bank of Japan hike we have been tracking, and a cheap yen keeps the carry funding that feeds the American technology boom flowing. Third, private credit. Financial Times analysis puts stress signals back to levels last seen in 2017 as troubled loans swell. That matters because private credit is the marginal lender to the data-centre build. Fourth, evidence the cycle is still hot: Singapore's non-oil exports rose twenty-four percent in July on AI demand. Fifth, Wednesday brings the UK July inflation print, expected near three percent and driven by energy bills, an import-price supply shock rather than demand. Watch cable.
