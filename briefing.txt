Weather. Right. Yesterday I told you Thursday was six millimetres of rain. It is eleven point three. I nearly doubled it overnight, so whatever coat you picked on the strength of six, upgrade it. Today is a proper soaking: ten to eighteen degrees, cloud the colour of wet concrete all day, and wind up to six metres a second driving it sideways into your face. Take the actual umbrella, not the one that inverts. And here is the genuinely annoying part. This is the only wet day of the week. From tomorrow it dries and warms, twenty-one, then twenty-three by Monday. One soggy day, and it is yours. Sorry.

Politics. One. The United States held its first-ever midterm party convention, in Dallas. Trump promised every American adult five thousand dollars if Republicans hold Congress in November; no funding mechanism was named, and earlier cash pledges never arrived. The battleground Senate nominees speaking avoided tariffs and the Iran war. Two. As previously flagged, Merz cancelled a call with Trump after Trump celebrated the far-right AfD's Saxony-Anhalt win, and accused the party of seeking ethnic cleansing; the small left-populist BSW's five seats are still the only arithmetic keeping it out of office.

Conflict. Hormuz, previously flagged, saw the largest attack on shipping since the war began. After American forces sank five Iranian tankers on Tuesday, Iran struck ten vessels near the strait, two warships and eight tankers, and fired ballistic missiles at an American base in Jordan. Brent settled at a hundred and one dollars, up three point four percent, its highest close since late May. The International Atomic Energy Agency referred Iran to the United Nations Security Council for the first time in twenty years, over uranium traces at undeclared sites. Trump says the war ends straight after November's midterms and oil stays high until it does; his own advisers expect it to run his whole term. Ukraine. Kyiv struck the Yamalo-Nenets gas region for the first time, two thousand eight hundred kilometres out, putting Russia's gas heartland inside the strike campaign.

International. Three to be aware of: a ferry fire off Palawan in the Philippines, five dead and eighty-six missing of a hundred and thirty-four aboard; a nationwide blackout in Malawi; thirteen dead in an avalanche in southern Russia. On the settlement bans, France and Canada have joined Britain, all twenty-seven European Union states now call the settlements illegal, and Israel has ordered Britain's East Jerusalem consulate shut in thirty days. Washington has taken no position. Serbia's snap election is confirmed for the twenty-fifth of October, and India hosts this weekend's summit of the BRICS emerging-market bloc.

Economy. The Federal Reserve first, because the consumer price index lands tomorrow. The mechanism: the Treasury announced a six billion dollar buyback of its own debt, retiring older bonds to compress the long end without touching policy rates. The intent was to signal official support for long-dated Treasuries and pull the term premium down. It failed inside a day. Ten-year yields rose to their highest in nearly three years, because six billion is trivial against issuance, and a buyback funded by short-dated bills is a duration swap, not a cut in supply, so the market read it as an admission that the long end is stressed rather than a fix. Read-through: with Brent above a hundred, the market prices a hike at next week's Federal Open Market Committee. Waller's condition stands, a hold if tomorrow's print cools and a hike if it does not, and the oil shock loads it the wrong way. The argument is unchanged and unresolved: the Phillips-curve reading says a tight labour market generalises an energy shock, so hike; the supply-shock reading says that is tightening into a relative-price move and buys a recession for nothing. American and Chinese borrowing costs have now diverged to the widest on record, which accelerates the capital-flow shift between them. Two Bank of Japan board members said rates must keep rising, one warning of price risks that could force rapid hikes, which revives the repatriation question. British petrol rose five pence in a week, the sharpest since April.

Business. As previously flagged, American import bans on Canadian alcohol, dairy and motorcycles are now in force. On the artificial intelligence build-out, the Justice Department is probing Nvidia's licensing deal with chip start-up Groq, and Nvidia announced a large Australian data-centre expansion. Kioxia rejected an SK Hynix tie-up and promised to ease memory price rises, the first supplier to talk the squeeze down. Channel 4 is cutting a quarter of its staff.

Deals. Spire Healthcare, Britain's largest private hospital operator, accepted a one billion pound private-equity takeover, its board citing tax rises as it quits the market. Silver Lake is merging two French software groups in a ten billion euro deal. Analog Devices buys Alif Semiconductor for one and a third billion dollars cash, acquiring edge processors rather than building them. The pattern that matters is the pace of private-equity buyouts of listed British companies, a steady drain of mid-caps off the London market.

Science and technology. One. The claim that a model solved a Navier-Stokes problem is contested on plagiarism grounds; a researcher says his session was scraped and his career threatened. Two. Gadgets. Apple's first foldable, the iPhone Duo, has a seven-point-six-inch inner screen, costs one thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine dollars and ships on the twenty-third of October. Then the ratchet: iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max start a hundred dollars up on their predecessors, at eleven hundred and ninety-nine and twelve hundred and ninety-nine, and older models rose a hundred too. That is the memory squeeze reaching the shelf. Nintendo's first special-edition Switch 2, a Zelda machine, is five hundred and twenty dollars on the nineteenth of October.

Energy. August was the hottest month ever recorded globally, ocean temperatures at record highs, capping western Europe's warmest summer; the United Nations says the one-and-a-half-degree limit is about to be passed. Against that, a survey finds most Chinese provinces now cutting coal use. California is reconsidering its nuclear ban, weighing a Diablo Canyon extension and new reactors.

Health. A first-of-its-kind narcolepsy drug treats the root cause rather than the sleepiness; the researchers who found the underlying orexin system took this year's Lasker award. Bangladesh's measles outbreak has passed a thousand child deaths; Kenya's forty-three-day nurses' strike is linked to five hundred and twenty-two.

Built environment. American data-centre planning cooled in August while every other construction sector accelerated, the first wobble in that pipeline and one to watch against capital-expenditure guidance.

Law. The Tate brothers were refused bail, held in Miami pending extradition to Britain on rape charges. A judge blocked Missouri from using a redrawn congressional map in November, freezing that redistricting until voters rule.

Society. International school testing shows Latin America falling back, Argentina at its worst since 2006, part of a global slide. In Kenya, previously flagged, the order closing foreign-owned businesses has left Burundian traders in limbo despite the amnesty.
