Turning to conflict and security. It caps almost a year of negotiation, driven by alarm at Iranian and Israeli operations across the region, and Erdogan has said it's open to other friendly nations joining. Now to the economy, where the real substance sits today. America's July jobs report was weak: employers cut twenty-three thousand positions rather than adding them, and the previous two months were revised down by a further hundred and three thousand, pulling the 2026 monthly average down to just sixty thousand - a labour market, in one economist's phrase, "stuck in second gear." Unemployment actually ticked down to 4.1 per cent, but only because hundreds of thousands of people left the workforce entirely, not because hiring picked up. Here's the analytical wrinkle worth sitting with: this doesn't weaken the case for a Federal Reserve rate cut nearly as much as you'd expect, because the Fed's own reading is that current inflation - which has now overshot its two per cent target for five straight years - is being driven by tariffs and the energy-price shock from the Iran war, not by a tight labour market. That's a supply-side inflation story rather than the classic Phillips-curve trade-off between unemployment and prices, and it means weak jobs numbers don't automatically buy the Fed room to ease, because slack in the labour market isn't judged to be what's pushing prices up in the first place. The new Fed chair, Kevin Warsh, has explicitly pledged to make price stability his priority, which points to a more hawkish reaction function than markets may be pricing in; next week's inflation print is now the pivotal data point. Layered on top of that is a fresh institutional fight: Donald Trump has revived his effort to remove Fed governor Lisa Cook, months after the Supreme Court ruled in June that she could stay in post while the underlying case is resolved - a renewed test of central bank independence that markets will start pricing as a variable of its own, separate from the data. On currencies, the Financial Times is calling out what it frames as America's financial weak spot: coordinated intervention to support the Japanese yen, which jumped against the dollar overnight, is being read as exposing underlying dollar fragility, and traders now expect the Bank of Japan to raise rates as soon as September rather than waiting until December.
