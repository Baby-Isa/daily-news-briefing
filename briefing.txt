Turning to conflict and security. The Iran war is now five months old. On to economy and markets, starting with the Federal Reserve, since that's the single most consequential institution for this portfolio. Fed chair Kevin Warsh was installed by Trump with an explicit mandate to cut rates, but he's boxed in: core inflation on the Fed's preferred measure, personal consumption expenditures, is still running at close to twice the two per cent target, driven partly by the Iran war's effect on energy prices and by the accumulating drag of tariffs. Warsh built his reputation as an inflation hawk, so this is a genuine contradiction - he's under White House pressure to loosen policy into a backdrop where the fundamentals argue the opposite, and his communication so far, notably last week's press conference, has itself become a source of market volatility. This is a live illustration of the central-bank-credibility problem: markets price not just what a central bank does but whether they believe what it says, and doubt on the second front has been showing up in bond and currency pricing. On the semiconductor and Taiwan side, which matters here given Nvidia is the largest single-name exposure once you look through the funds: Intel's shares have quadrupled off their low as Washington leans on it as a domestic chip champion, but reporting suggests it remains unproven as a genuine manufacturing rival to TSMC - worth remembering given how much of the sector's current premium is a bet on reshoring actually working, and that bet sits right alongside this week's China export controls and the new Taiwan arms package as reminders that the chip supply chain is now inseparable from great-power politics.
