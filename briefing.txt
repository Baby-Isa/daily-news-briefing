Weather. Eleven degrees when you walk out, nineteen by the afternoon, cloud all the way through and just under three millimetres of rain smeared so thin you will never once see it fall. Eight degrees of swing. Eight! You will pick a coat for one of those numbers and be wrong for about nine hours. The rest of the week sits in the same grey rut, highs pinned between nineteen and twenty. Then Thursday. Twelve millimetres. And it is climbing: seven and a bit when we looked on Tuesday, twelve now, still rising. That is not drizzle, that is a soaking with a run-up. Build the week around Thursday.

Politics. One. The US House voted a fifth time to rein in the president's war powers, directing him to halt the Iran war or get Congressional approval, seven Republicans joining; a Republican separately forced an impeachment vote on Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth over the same war. Neither binds him; both put a hostile chamber around the war seven weeks before the midterms. Two. Britain. Wage growth of three point nine percent sets April's triple-lock rise, taking the state pension above thirteen thousand pounds; pensioners with no other income will owe no tax on it, so the frozen personal allowance does the work everywhere else. Three. Merz cancelled a New York trip to shore up his own party before the Mecklenburg-Vorpommern and Berlin state elections on the twentieth of September, the next hard reading on the Alternative for Germany.

Hormuz crossings are still four a day. Two. International. On the Canada thread, the Europe pivot hit its first wall: European Union governments rebuffed Carney's call for a unique alliance of middle powers against Washington and Beijing, days after von der Leyen agreed to attend his Brussels speech.

Economy. The Federal Open Market Committee's meeting ends today. The announcement is at two in the afternoon New York time, six this evening here, after this brief closes; as promised on Tuesday, the result reaches you tomorrow. The mechanism: the federal funds target, a quarter-point rise expected, from a committee a sitting president has publicly demanded cut instead. The intent: stop an oil shock setting into an inflation expectation, by tightening enough that fuel costs cannot pass into wages. The disagreement is genuine: the Phillips-curve reading says the labour market is tight enough to generalise the shock, so tighten; the supply-shock reading says no interest rate reopens a Saudi pipeline, so you buy the output loss and get no disinflation for it. Read-through: a rise firms the dollar, weakens sterling against dollar-denominated holdings and lifts the discount rate on the artificial-intelligence capital-expenditure trade, while a hold reads as capitulation to the president. Watch the dot plot as closely as the rate. Two things moved overnight. A poll of academic economists says the Fed should raise, and should defy the president in doing so. And the long end is moving without the Fed: the ten-year Treasury yield touched five point zero four percent on Tuesday, its highest since two thousand and seven, while the Treasury secretary defended a buyback visibly not holding yields down. Term premium, not policy rate. Behind it, the supply side no central bank reaches: Saudi Arabia has cancelled crude cargoes to Europe with its East-West pipeline shut and Yanbu halted, is routing barrels to Asian refiners via Oman, and up to four percent of world supply is at risk into October.

Business. The artificial-intelligence industry split in public. The heads of Nvidia and Meta rejected the coordinated slowdown proposed by the bosses of OpenAI, Anthropic and SpaceX, Jensen Huang saying no new laws are needed; Sam Altman said the world is right to be afraid but should trust the firms building it. The politics run the other way: sixty-one percent of likely American voters oppose building data centres, and senators of both parties are drafting limits. OpenAI is weighing a round at a one point two trillion dollar valuation before flotation.

Deals. One. Grab, Southeast Asia's ride-hailing and payments app, is buying sixty percent of buy-now-pay-later lender Atome for one and a half billion dollars, full control to follow: consumer credit is where the region's superapps now compete. Two. Brookfield is taking Reliance Worldwide, the Australian-listed maker of SharkBite plumbing fittings, private for two point eight billion Australian dollars. Three. Britain's GSK is paying up to seven hundred and fifty million dollars for global rights to a Chinese myeloma drug: Western pharma again licensing its pipeline out of China rather than discovering it.

Science. SpaceX will attempt Starship's first orbital flight on the twenty-second of September, deploying twenty-six Starlink satellites over about ten hours; every flight so far has been suborbital. NASA's Roman telescope, the first observatory built for refuelling in orbit, has doubled its working life to twenty-two years on one precise engine burn. Agility's new humanoid warehouse robot stops and squats when a person approaches, which lets it work outside a safety cage.

Gadgets. Canon announced the EOS R8 Mark II, a full-frame mirrorless camera that finally adds in-body stabilisation, the original's biggest omission, plus four-K sixty and weather sealing. Just under nineteen hundred dollars body only, twenty-one hundred with the kit lens, shipping late October; the first R8 launched at fifteen hundred, so stabilisation costs four hundred. Microsoft pushed an emergency fix for last week's Windows eleven update, which was crashing AMD graphics drivers and hanging File Explorer, and has called a Windows and Surface event for the seventh of October, its first in over two years.

Energy. The Climate Change Committee has told ministers a third Heathrow runway should be approved only if airlines pay to remove the carbon dioxide they emit. On current policy, flying reaches eighty percent of all UK emissions by twenty fifty, aviation having doubled since nineteen ninety while every other sector fell.

Health. Pennsylvania has reported a fourth measles death in a month, an unvaccinated eighteen-year-old who developed brain inflammation; US measles deaths are at a decades high.

Society. The Kennedy Center will close for renovations after a judge again blocked the president's name being restored to it; the two hundred and fifty-seven million dollars Congress voted for repairs had been conditioned on that renaming.

Sport. Nairobi will host the twenty twenty-nine World Athletics Championships, the first time athletics' flagship event has gone to Africa.
