Economy. The story is the long end, everywhere at once. Japan's benchmark yield touched three percent for the first time in thirty years and Australia's ten-year hit a fifteen-year high, extending the G7 debt-service squeeze we flagged Sunday.

The mechanism: Bessent has publicly told Japan to raise rates and called time on big fiscal stimulus, so the Bank of Japan is being pushed to tighten into an already selling bond market. The intended effect is currency policy without a tariff, because a narrower rate gap lifts the yen and erodes Japan's export price advantage. The second-order risk is the interesting part: a hike raises debt service on a stock above two hundred percent of output and can unwind the yen carry trade, forcing Japanese institutions to repatriate and sell Treasuries, which is precisely what the American long end cannot absorb. Monetarists read the global move as inflation risk being repriced; the fiscal-dominance camp reads it as too much duration chasing too few buyers. Read-through: if it is the term premium rising, the discount rate on long-duration AI capex names moves against you before earnings do. Cost of capital, not demand, is the swing factor.
