Politics and government. In Westminster, Prime Minister Andy Burnham's government spent the week absorbing two of its trickiest subjects, immigration and prisons, at once. Norfolk is into a third night of disorder in Thetford, tied to anti-immigration protests targeting people believed to be asylum seekers; police have made further arrests, an officer was bitten, and separate violent clashes broke out in South Wales with two people in critical condition. The Conservatives, under Kemi Badenoch, used the moment to pledge a ban on foreign nationals from social housing, claiming it would free roughly two hundred and thirty thousand homes, while separately facing criticism for giving an advisory role to a former neo-Nazi. On the left flank, the more interesting structural story is the Green Party's membership, which has reportedly quadrupled under its new leader and is closing in on Labour's, whose own membership has more than halved over the past decade to around two hundred and sixty-five thousand: a genuine realignment story in British politics, not just a bad opinion poll. In Washington, the birthright citizenship fight escalated again: President Trump signed two fresh executive orders restricting citizenship for children born on US soil to certain parents, reviving a policy the Supreme Court had rejected only five weeks earlier, which sets up an immediate and serious constitutional confrontation over how far a president can narrow a constitutional guarantee by decree. In Michigan, progressive Abdul El-Sayed won the Democratic Senate primary over an establishment, AIPAC-backed rival, and will face Republican Mike Rogers in November; moderate Democrats are reportedly readying a fifteen-million-dollar campaign against the party's ascendant left, a genuine intra-party fight worth watching into the midterms. And a programming note on three stories from well outside the US, UK and Middle East, chosen because they matter structurally rather than because they're loud: Sudan's civil war has just seen its most significant shift in months, the Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo is now the fastest-spreading in the country's history, and the migrant emergency in Spain's North African exclave of Ceuta has become a genuine test of European Union cohesion. Conflict and security. The Iran war grinds on without resolution: Trump says he thinks it will end "pretty soon" and denies any US munitions shortage, even as reporting continues to suggest depleted stocks of harder-to-replace weapons. The more concrete movement is around the Strait of Hormuz, where Iran and Oman appear close to a deal that would partially reopen shipping, though Iran's parliament is separately drafting legislation to bar US and Israeli vessels outright and charge tolls to everyone else; actual vessel traffic through the strait has already dropped by roughly a third week on week, which tells you the market isn't waiting for the paperwork. Yemen's Houthis had one of their deadliest days of the war, killing at least fifty-eight Saudi-backed government troops and wounding civilians in Saudi Arabia itself, and Riyadh is warning of imminent, coordinated attacks alongside Iran-backed Iraqi militias. Underlining how deep the covert side of this war runs, Israel's Mossad chief has fired two senior officials over a failed plot to topple Iran's government. In Sudan, now in its fourth year of civil war, the Sudanese military recaptured a strategically important highway from the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces, its most significant battlefield gain in months, though the RSF appears to be adapting by leaning harder on tribal mobilisation and foreign-backed drone warfare rather than folding. In Ukraine, now on day one thousand six hundred and twenty-six, President Zelenskyy says Kyiv will build its own long-range missiles the way it scaled up drone production, wanting concrete results by year end, as Western allies grow more reluctant to hand over scarce interceptor missiles. Separately, Russian strikes have effectively frozen Ukrainian Black Sea shipping again, echoing the 2022 blockade, cutting Ukraine's grain exports by roughly a third and threatening a repeat of the global food-price shock that followed the last one; Ukraine's agriculture ministry puts the direct revenue loss at one and a half to three billion dollars this year alone. And in Germany, an explosive drone was found near a Ukrainian cargo plane carrying ammunition at Leipzig airport; investigators are examining foreign state involvement, and Lithuania has separately warned that Russia may stage a false-flag attack using Ukrainian-made drones against Baltic infrastructure.

International affairs. The story with the most structural weight this week is the migrant crisis in Ceuta, the Spanish exclave on Morocco's coast: somewhere between seventy-two and seventy-eight thousand people crossed the border in a matter of days, roughly a hundred people are reported to have died in the process, thousands of unaccompanied minors are still sleeping rough, and Washington has waded in, affirming Ceuta as Spanish territory while sharply criticising Madrid's Pedro Sánchez for what it called a "shameful refusal" to defend it. The European Union's own migration chief admits the bloc "can't depend on neighbours" for border control, which is a fairly blunt admission of how exposed this leaves EU policy generally. There's a geopolitical undertow here too: Washington has been warming to Morocco over Western Sahara at the same time Spain has drifted further from Washington and Israel over Gaza, so Ceuta is functioning as a proxy for several other rifts at once. Elsewhere in Europe, the Rhine's record-low water levels are now a genuine economic story, disrupting the barge traffic that much of German industry depends on, adding cost and risk to supply chains already strained by a brutal summer of heat and wildfire, and France has announced a nationwide blackout drill for 2027 in response to the same pressures. In East Asia, Beijing has responded to the prospect of a Trump-Xi summit by simultaneously flexing legal and trade countermeasures, sanctioning US entities and tightening drone-export controls, while also buying up more American soybeans, a fairly transparent goodwill gesture ahead of Xi's expected US visit next month; analysts are calling the tit-for-tat measures a "speed bump" rather than a summit-breaker. Argentina has renewed its nineteen-billion-dollar currency swap with China for another five years, brushing off a year of American pressure to drop it. And Taiwan's drone-procurement programme is bogged down in budget wrangling badly enough that the island could end the year without a single new large-scale contract for domestically produced combat drones, which matters given how central drone warfare has become to every deterrence conversation about a Taiwan contingency.

Economy and markets, where the biggest story of the day is arguably at the Federal Reserve. Kevin Warsh, now Fed chair, is driving a reform push that's drawing real pushback, which the Financial Times argues is unwarranted; whichever side you land on, the fact that Fed leadership itself has become a live political fight is the headline, not any single data point. Layered on top of that is a genuinely unusual currency story: Washington intervened directly in markets, selling euros to prop up the yen, in what's being described as a historic move, and apparently only briefed the European Central Bank's Christine Lagarde after the fact. Unilateral intervention that skips the normal G7 courtesy of a heads-up to the counterparty central bank is a real break from convention, and doing it via the euro rather than simply the dollar raises the question of what Washington is actually trying to manage, presumably an overly weak yen that's straining Japanese households and Asian trade balances, rather than the dollar's own value. Japan's own data cuts both ways: household spending fell 3.3 percent year on year in June against persistent inflation, well below expectations, even as a Reuters poll still expects a third straight quarter of growth on solid domestic demand, and Japanese carmakers now expect the yen to hold near its post-intervention level rather than resume its slide. On trade, Trump has imposed a fifteen percent tariff plus a price floor on polysilicon, the key material in both solar panels and semiconductors, aimed squarely at Chinese dominance of that supply chain; given how central Taiwan and the semiconductor chain are to this portfolio, that's worth tracking beyond the headline. Tokyo's stock exchange is also about to cull more than six hundred of its smallest, least-liquid listed companies from the Topix index over the next two years, a genuinely large structural clean-up of the Japanese market. And gold's bull case keeps building quietly in the background: Beijing has now added to its reserves for twenty straight months running, and analysts are calling the upside "explosive" as Hong Kong pushes to become a bigger physical trading hub, part of the broader central-bank de-dollarisation trend worth keeping an eye on even without a single big one-day move to point to. Domestically in the US, the Senate Finance Committee held hearings this week on how to keep Social Security solvent as the trust fund's shortfall approaches; the arithmetic is simple, the politics genuinely are not, and nothing was resolved.

That's split Silicon Valley and Washington: Nvidia, Microsoft, Google, Meta and OpenAI have all now signed an open letter defending the value of open models even as some in Washington see Chinese open-weight dominance as a security risk. Health and medicine. The story that deserves real weight here is the Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo, now the fastest-spreading in the country's history and the second-largest Ebola outbreak ever recorded, with close to four thousand confirmed cases and more than one thousand eight hundred deaths. The World Health Organization is calling for an intensified response as frontline health workers in Ituri province, the epicentre, go on strike over unpaid wages, hospital usage in hotspot areas has dropped forty-two percent as people avoid facilities out of fear of catching the virus there, and the virus has now reached displacement camps. That combination, a resurgent epidemic and a collapsing local health workforce, is a genuinely serious structural crisis, not just a headline count. On the science side, a promising anti-inflammatory heart drug from Novo Nordisk has failed in trials, and its failure is now genuinely reopening the question of how central inflammation actually is to causing heart disease in the first place, rather than being a downstream marker of it; that's a real scientific dispute worth watching rather than a settled matter. More positively, the first mRNA-based flu vaccine, from Moderna, has been approved for adults fifty and over ahead of this coming flu season, and a large long-term study suggests nearly half of dementia cases may be linked to modifiable risks such as smoking, high blood pressure and cholesterol via damage to the brain's blood vessels, a genuinely actionable finding rather than an abstract one. Separately, US authorities are shutting down a Kentucky organ-donation group after allegations it pressured staff to retrieve organs from patients who were showing signs of recovery, and Dr Anthony Fauci was voted into contempt of Congress by a Senate committee for refusing to answer questions about the pandemic response, a case legal scholars say is unusual for pitting his Fifth Amendment rights against the reach of a presidential pardon.
