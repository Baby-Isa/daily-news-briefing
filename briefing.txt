Weather.

Twelve degrees at dawn, nineteen by mid-afternoon, clear sky, rainfall of exactly nothing. So you will leave the house in a coat and carry the bloody thing round all day like a man holding someone else's shopping. Layers. Jacket off at eleven.

And the week does not break. Six consecutive days with essentially no rain, three of them cloudless, warming to twenty-three on Tuesday. No wet day hiding in the middle, no cold snap. Late September, in England, handing out a fortnight of holiday weather for free. Enjoy every minute, and keep one eye on the sky, because nothing this generous arrives without a bill.

Politics and government.

One. Germany votes today. Berlin and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, roughly three point eight million voters, with the hard-right Alternative for Germany pressing hard in the north. Chancellor Merz's own standing rides on that northern result.

Two. The White House confiscated press passes from three news organisations on Saturday, making good on the president's ban. Seven weeks from the midterms, that is control of the room rather than of a story.

Three. Trump will create an artificial intelligence force and name a czar for it. No remit, no authority, no detail, and offered instead of regulation.

Four. Copenhagen calls the binding American security arrangement over Greenland no threat to Danish sovereignty, but has published no terms, and Greenlanders say the silence is the problem.

Five. Britain. Badenoch told Burnham to cut welfare and fund defence with it. Michael Marra is the new Scottish Labour leader, and he backs both the Rosebank and Jackdaw fields.

Conflict and security.

One. The Houthis fired a ballistic missile at Riyadh, the first time the Saudi capital has been targeted since this front opened. The coalition says it was intercepted; explosions and smoke were reported at King Khalid airport, and the group claims strikes on Aramco facilities at Yanbu too. Washington warns of rapid escalation and Trump cut short a Camp David weekend without explanation. Turkey has offered to meet Saudi military needs under the Mecca pact it shares with Pakistan, the first sign that arrangement does anything at all.

Two. Russia's parliamentary vote ends today, day two run with observers barred and turnout claimed above forty per cent; Kyiv and the European Commission call the inclusion of annexed regions illegal.

Three. Washington approved a two point six eight billion dollar air-defence sale to Ukraine, and Zelensky has authorised new long-range operations. Russia's air campaign is now aimed at the economy rather than the front: warehouses, steel plants, ports and railways, stripping Kyiv of over one and a half billion dollars in tax revenue.

International affairs.

Three to be aware of: at least forty-eight dead and about a hundred and eighty ill in Ondo State, Nigeria, from a methanol-laced spirit; thirty-seven suspected illegal miners dead in custody in Niger State, cause disputed; and Cuba dark again in another national blackout.

Leaders reach New York this week for the United Nations General Assembly, Trump at the rostrum Tuesday, Kenya's Ruto carrying an Africa-financing-Africa pitch — and, behind him, Uganda's Museveni publicly accusing the government-to-government fuel import scheme of swindling Kampala, the arrangement that sets Kenyan pump prices. Abbas, refused an American visa a second year running, addresses the hall by video.

Economy and markets.

One. Trump signed the Russia and Iran sanctions act on Friday, ahead of the deadline this brief was watching. The mechanism: statutory sanctions on Russia expanded, Iranian sanctions extended to twenty thirty-one, and presidential authority to tariff any country buying Russian crude at up to a hundred per cent, with thirty days to act and broad power to waive. The intent is to make Russian barrels cost more than the discount is worth, a secondary-sanctions squeeze routed through the tariff schedule rather than the banks. The risks cut both ways: India and China are the targets, and Beijing, the largest buyer, already reserves the right to take all necessary measures days before Xi reaches Washington; and applied in full it pulls several million barrels a day from a market where Europe is already short, raising the price the policy exists to deny Moscow. The read-through: it is a tariff, so it lands on American import prices too, and it tightens the energy condition the Bank of England attached to its hold at three and three-quarter per cent. One counterweight, running against the shortage story: an American commander reports Hormuz shipments at a six-month high.

Two. The crash question is now asked openly: artificial intelligence capital spending increasingly funded by debt rather than cash flow, government borrowing costs at multi-decade highs, a war premium in oil. That is one story, not three. The long end prices the debt the capital spending needs, so a term-premium move does the damage before any earnings miss does.

Three. Saudi Arabia has quit the China-led cross-border central bank currency platform, withdrawing last year. Trivial in flow, loud in signal: the largest holder of petrodollars declining Beijing's alternative to them.

Business and industry.

One. Tata Steel has asked the government for fresh funding as the Port Talbot transformation slips, the second British steelmaker in a week whose survival plan its overseers do not believe.

Two. China is now Rwanda's largest export market, twenty-six per cent of exports in the first quarter against under five a year earlier, after Beijing dropped tariffs on almost all Rwandan goods. A tariff cut rerouting a country's trade inside a year, though exporters say the constraint is now volume, not access.

Science and technology.

One. Starship's first orbital attempt has slipped: SpaceX now targets the twenty-eighth of September, not the twenty-second, for multiple orbits across ten hours.

Two. Anthropic has appointed Accenture to run third-party safety evaluations of its models, the first concrete step behind its chief executive's stated intent to slow development down.

Three. Chariklo, a body some two hundred and fifty kilometres across beyond Saturn, has two rings, and they have visibly changed across a decade. Ring systems are not permanent furniture.

Gadgets and consumer tech. Nothing shipped, changed price or was discontinued today.

Energy and environment.

Germany has cut fuel duty as Middle East supply pushes pump prices up: a fiscal offset to an imported price shock, and the pattern to watch as European governments go into winter with the Bank of England's energy condition still live.

Health and medicine.

The Jehovah's Witnesses have partly eased their blood prohibition, permitting blood-derived products while keeping the ban on whole-blood transfusion. That changes surgical consent for millions. In the Democratic Republic of Congo, Ebola vaccination trials have begun for frontline health workers as the outbreak reaches new areas.

Law and justice. An American appeals court has restricted deportations to third countries, letting migrants contest removal to places they are not from. It is headed for the Supreme Court.

Culture and sport. The Asian Games opened in Nagoya, Japan's Takumi Hojo taking the first gold in the triathlon.

Special interests.

Arsenal lost. Yesterday's trip to Brighton, flagged here as upcoming, ended three-nil: the hundred per cent start gone, a nine-match league winning run with it. Pascal Gross from distance on thirty-one minutes, Charalampos Kostoulas just before the interval, Chema Andres heading in a corner soon after. Arteta said Brighton deserved to win and that the side put themselves in trouble from the first whistle. Squad level: Timber started and came off at half-time alongside Guimaraes, both tactical rather than fitness, Rice shifted to right-back, and no new injuries emerged. Saliba's back is still counted in months, late November the estimate. Elsewhere Saturday: Aston Villa won three-two at Tottenham, and Coventry won one-nil at Nottingham Forest for a first top-flight win in twenty-five years. Manchester City host Sunderland today.

Aviation: a turbine durability kit is certified for the engine family on the Boeing seven-three-seven MAX, its makers claiming it doubles time on wing in hot and dusty conditions. An operating-cost change across the fleet, not a specification tweak.

Longevity: semaglutide extended median lifespan in female mice and improved several ageing measures. Mice, females only; the headline is running ahead of the evidence.

Chainlink and OriginTrail: nothing new today.

Editorial picks.

The Economist's Weekend Intelligence carries Shire Folk, from last weekend: how some of the most powerful people in America came to see Britain, and Islam, through the eyes of a fringe football hooligan. Worth it for the transmission mechanism rather than the subject.

And a Pulitzer-supported dispatch from inside Iran, filed this week by a correspondent based in Tehran: nearly seven months of war measured not in strikes but in soaring prices, lengthening fuel queues and a leadership hardening as sanctions bite. Ask and I will expand either.
